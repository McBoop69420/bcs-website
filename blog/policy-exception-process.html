Policy Exceptions: How to Approve Risk Without Losing Control

Published September 1, 2026. Written for all SMBs.

Exceptions Aren't the Problem

Every organization eventually hits a policy it can't meet on schedule. A legacy application can't support MFA. A vendor needs a firewall rule wider than the standard allows. A department needs a shared mailbox the access policy technically forbids. None of that is unusual, and none of it is a failure on its own.

The risk comes from exceptions that are never written down: the approval buried in an email thread, the gap everyone on the IT team knows about, the “temporary” workaround that's now three years old. When an auditor, examiner, or insurer asks why a control isn't in place, “we talked about it” is not an answer.

What Every Exception Record Should Capture

A good exception record fits on one page. It needs to answer five questions.

Why is the exception necessary?

State the business need in plain language. If the reason is “it would be inconvenient,” that's worth knowing before anyone signs off.

What could go wrong while it's in place?

Describe the specific risk, not a generic one. “An attacker with a stolen password could reach the loan system without a second factor” is something a decision maker can weigh. “Increased security risk” isn't.

What's reducing the risk in the meantime?

List the compensating controls: extra monitoring, network restrictions, limited user counts, more frequent reviews. If there aren't any, say so. That's important information for the person approving it.

Who accepted the risk?

The approver should be someone with authority over the risk, not just the person who asked for the exception. For larger exceptions, that may mean a committee or senior management, not the IT manager.

When does it end?

Every exception needs an expiration date. On that date it gets fixed, renewed with a fresh approval, or retired. An exception with no end date is just an undocumented policy change.

Watch for Exceptions That Keep Renewing

Review open exceptions on a regular cadence, quarterly works for most organizations, and pay attention to the ones that keep coming back. A repeat renewal usually means one of three things: the policy is unrealistic and should change, the underlying system needs investment, or leadership needs to formally accept the residual risk. All three are legitimate outcomes. Renewing on autopilot isn't.
