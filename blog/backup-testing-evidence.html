Backup Testing Evidence: Proving You Can Actually Recover

Published September 29, 2026. Written for regulated institutions.

A Successful Backup Isn't a Successful Recovery

Nightly backup reports with green checkmarks are reassuring, but they only prove that data was copied somewhere. They don't prove the data is usable, that systems can be rebuilt from it, or that recovery fits inside the time the business can tolerate. Ransomware has made that difference painfully concrete: plenty of organizations have discovered during an incident that their backups were incomplete, corrupted, or reachable by the attacker.

Examiners increasingly ask for evidence that restores are actually tested, that results are reviewed, and that failures are fixed.

What Good Evidence Shows

  • Scope: which systems, data sets, and applications were included, and how that ties back to your business impact analysis.
  • Method: what was restored, where it was restored to, and who confirmed it actually worked. A file that opens is a different test than an application that processes transactions.
  • Timing: how long recovery took, measured against your recovery time and recovery point objectives.
  • Backup integrity: confirmation that at least one copy is offline or immutable and couldn't have been altered by someone with administrative access to your network.
  • Exceptions: failed restores, missing systems, slow recoveries, and dependencies nobody had documented.
  • Sign-off: management review of the results, with follow-up items moved to your remediation tracker.

Test the Systems That Matter Most

You don't need to restore everything every time. Rotate through systems on a schedule, but make sure your most critical applications are tested at least annually, and test again after major changes such as a core conversion, a new backup product, or a move to a cloud provider. If a third party hosts a critical system, ask how they test recovery and request the results.

Use the Same Template Every Time

Consistency is what turns individual tests into a story. When every test is recorded the same way, trends become visible: recovery times getting longer, the same dependency failing twice, a system that's never been tested. That's exactly what the board and examiners want to see, and it's much easier to show when the record already exists.
