Cyber Insurance Questionnaires: Answer Them Like Someone Will Check

Published October 27, 2026. Written for all SMBs.

The Application Is Part of the Policy

Renewal season usually brings a security questionnaire from your cyber insurance carrier. It's tempting to treat it as paperwork: someone in finance or operations fills it out, checks “yes” on most of the boxes, and sends it back. But those answers aren't a formality. The carrier priced your policy based on them, and they're typically incorporated into the policy itself.

That matters most at the worst possible moment. After a ransomware attack or a business email compromise, the carrier's investigators will look at how the incident happened and compare it with what you said on the application. Insurers have denied claims and even sought to rescind policies when a stated control, MFA being the most common example, turned out not to be in place where the application said it was.

The Questions That Trip People Up

“Do you require MFA for all remote access?”

The word “all” is doing a lot of work. MFA on email and the VPN is a good start, but carriers often mean every path in: remote desktop tools, vendor support connections, cloud admin consoles, and the firewall's own management interface. One exception is enough to make a “yes” inaccurate.

“Do you maintain offline or immutable backups?”

A backup that sits on a network share or syncs continuously to the cloud may be reachable by the same attacker who encrypted your servers. If you can't say exactly where the isolated copy lives and when it was last restored successfully, find out before answering.

“Do you have endpoint detection and response on all devices?”

Traditional antivirus usually isn't what the question is asking about, and coverage gaps are common: servers that were never enrolled, laptops that dropped off, machines that belong to a department IT doesn't manage.

“Do you verify payment and banking changes out of band?”

Funds transfer fraud coverage often depends on this one. A documented call-back procedure that staff actually follow, using a phone number already on file and not one from the email requesting the change, is what makes the answer true.

How to Answer Accurately

  • Route each question to the person who knows. The person signing the application usually isn't the person who manages backups or MFA. Get answers from whoever operates the control.
  • Answer what's true today, not what's planned. If a control is in progress, say so. Most applications have room for explanation, and a qualified answer is far better than an inaccurate yes.
  • Keep the evidence. Save a copy of the submitted application along with screenshots or reports that support each key answer. If a claim is ever questioned, that file is what shows you answered in good faith.
  • Treat gaps as a to-do list. Every “no” or “partially” is a control the carrier thinks matters. Put them on your remediation tracker with owners and dates, and you'll have a better story at next year's renewal, often with a better premium.

Read the Policy, Too

While the application is in front of you, check the policy's notice requirements and whether the carrier expects you to use its panel of incident response firms and attorneys. Those details belong in your incident response plan, so nobody has to look them up during an incident.
