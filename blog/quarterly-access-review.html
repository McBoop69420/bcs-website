Quarterly Access Reviews: Keep Them Small Enough to Finish

Published October 20, 2026. Written for regulated institutions.

Why Access Reviews Fail

Most institutions know they should review user access regularly, and most have a review that technically happens. The common failure isn't skipping it. It's a review so large that managers rubber-stamp a spreadsheet of hundreds of entitlements they don't understand, and nothing changes. That produces evidence of a review without the benefit of one, and examiners have learned to spot it.

Start With the Highest Risk

A quarterly review doesn't need to cover every account in every system. It needs to cover the access that would hurt most if it were wrong.

  • Privileged accounts: domain administrators, core system administrators, and anyone who can change security settings or other users' access.
  • Dormant accounts: accounts with no recent logins or no clear owner, including service accounts nobody remembers creating.
  • Role changes: employees who moved to a new position but kept access from the old one. This is where access quietly accumulates.
  • Vendor and remote access: remote support accounts, portal users, and anything a third party can reach.

Cover lower-risk access on a slower rotation, semiannually or annually, so the full picture still gets reviewed over the course of a year.

Ask Managers the Right Question

“Is this access appropriate?” next to a list of cryptic permission names gets a yes every time. Translate entitlements into plain language, such as “can approve wire transfers” or “can edit member account records,” and ask managers to confirm each person still needs it for their current job. Make removal the easy answer.

The Review Isn't Done Until Changes Are Made

Track every removal and change to completion, and confirm it in the system, not just in the ticket. Keep a record of who reviewed what, what they approved, what was changed, and when the change was verified. Any access that stays despite being questionable should go through your exception process with an owner and an expiration date.

That record is what proves the review worked. It's also what you'll be asked for at your next exam.
