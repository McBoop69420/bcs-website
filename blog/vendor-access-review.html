Vendor Access Reviews: What to Check Before Renewal

Published November 10, 2026. Written for all SMBs.

Why Renewal Is the Right Moment

Vendor reviews usually center on the paperwork: the contract, the insurance certificate, the SOC report, the financials. Those matter, but they describe the vendor. They don't tell you who from that vendor can log in to your environment today. Access tends to accumulate quietly. A technician is added for a project that ends, a support account is created for an emergency and never removed, and a shared login becomes the way everyone gets in.

Contract renewal is a natural checkpoint because you already have the vendor's attention and some leverage. Asking for an access review at that moment feels routine rather than adversarial, and any cleanup can be written into the new term instead of negotiated later.

What to Review

Start from your side of the connection, not the vendor's description of it. Pull the actual list of vendor accounts and permissions from your own systems, then compare it to what the vendor says it needs.

  • Named accounts: confirm every active vendor user still works on your account and still needs access. Ask the vendor to confirm the same list from their end.
  • Privileged access: verify administrative roles, remote support tools, and emergency or break-glass accounts. These carry the most risk and deserve the closest look.
  • Shared credentials: identify any account several vendor staff use together and replace it with named accounts wherever the system allows.
  • Authentication: confirm multi-factor authentication and password requirements match what you expect of your own staff, not a weaker standard.
  • Connections and keys: list the VPN tunnels, API keys, service accounts, and integrations the vendor uses, and note who owns each one on your side.
  • Data reach: check what the vendor can actually see or export. Access that was sized for one project often covers far more than the current work needs.
  • Offboarding: test how quickly the vendor tells you when their staff change roles or leave, and how quickly access is removed when they do. The same gap described in employee offboarding applies to people who don't work for you.

Match Access to Risk

Not every vendor deserves the same depth of review. A vendor that can reach customer data, move money, or administer your systems warrants a full pass at every renewal. One that supplies office furniture does not. Tier your vendors by what they can touch, and spend the review effort where a compromise would hurt most. If you already keep a vendor inventory, add a column for the access each vendor holds so the renewal review starts with a list instead of a search.

Pair the access review with the assurance you already collect. A clean SOC report is a good sign about the vendor's controls, but it doesn't speak to the accounts they hold in your environment. Our guide to reviewing a SOC report covers the first half; this review covers the second.

Document the Decision

The review should end with a short record: what was checked, what changed, who approved continued access, and which exceptions remain open. A one-page summary per vendor is enough. That record becomes useful evidence during audits, exams, and incident reviews, and it saves the next reviewer from starting over.

Where something can't be fixed before the renewal date, record the exception, name an owner, and set a date to revisit it. An exception with an owner and a deadline is a managed risk. One without is just a gap you've agreed to forget.
