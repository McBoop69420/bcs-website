Incident Communications: What to Prepare Before the Phone Rings

Published October 13, 2026. Written for all SMBs.

Communications Break Down First

When an incident hits, technical response and communication have to happen at the same time. Most incident response plans spend pages on the technical side and a paragraph on the rest. That's backwards from how incidents actually feel: the first hours are dominated by questions like who needs to know, who's allowed to say what, and what we tell customers who are already calling.

Some of those decisions also have deadlines. Cyber insurance policies often require prompt notice before you engage outside help. Regulated institutions may have notification clocks as short as 36 hours. Customer notification laws vary by state. None of that is the moment to start looking up phone numbers.

What to Prepare Now

A contact tree that works offline

Leadership, IT, legal counsel, your cyber insurance carrier's claims line, key vendors, and, where relevant, regulators and law enforcement. Keep a copy outside your email and file servers. If those are what's down, you'll still need it.

A clear approval path

Decide in advance who drafts and who approves statements to employees, customers, the board, and the public. Name a backup for each. Under pressure, unclear approval is how organizations end up either silent for too long or saying too much too early.

Message templates

Short, pre-approved drafts for the most likely situations: a service disruption, a suspected data exposure, a vendor outage that affects you, and a holding statement for “we're investigating.” Templates won't be perfect, but editing a draft is much faster than writing one at 2 a.m.

An update cadence

Agree on how often leadership and the board get status updates during an incident, and in what form. Regular updates, even when there's little new to report, prevent the side conversations that pull responders off their work.

Test It

At least once a year, run a tabletop exercise that forces communication decisions, not just technical ones. Ask who calls the insurer, who approves the customer notice, and where the timeline of decisions is being recorded. The gaps you find in an hour of discussion are much cheaper than the ones you find during a real event.
