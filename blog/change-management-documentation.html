Change Management Documentation: The Overlooked Control Examiners Flag

Published August 18, 2026

The Control Usually Exists — the Record Doesn't

Most institutions already have a change management process: someone approves changes before they go to production, and someone tests them after. The gap examiners find isn't the process itself — it's proving the process was followed consistently, for every change, over the full exam period.

A verbal "we always get sign-off" doesn't hold up next to a sampled change with no ticket, no approver, and no rollback plan on file.

What a Defensible Change Record Includes

  • Request: what's changing, why, and who asked for it.
  • Risk and impact: systems affected, and whether it touches anything customer-facing or regulated.
  • Approval: who signed off, and evidence they had the authority to.
  • Testing: what was validated before deployment, and by whom.
  • Rollback plan: the specific steps to undo the change if it fails.
  • Post-implementation review: confirmation the change worked as intended.

Emergency changes need the same trail, just compressed — a same-day approval and a required after-the-fact review, not an exemption from documentation.

Where This Breaks Down in Practice

The most common gap is vendor-managed or SaaS changes: patches and configuration updates the vendor pushes on their own schedule. Examiners still expect your institution to track what changed, when, and whether it was reviewed — even if you didn't perform the change yourself.

The second most common gap is scope: a change ticketing tool exists, but it's only used for major projects. Routine changes — firewall rule updates, permission changes, server patching — skip the process entirely because they feel too small to bother with.

Keeping It Sustainable

Change management documentation fails when it's too heavy for routine work. A lightweight ticket template that captures the six items above, applied consistently even to small changes, will hold up far better under sampling than a detailed process that's only followed for the big ones.
